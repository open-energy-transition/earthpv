# Beyond the rooftops

A guest post by
[Tayyiba Shafiq](https://www.linkedin.com/in/tayyiba-shafiq/) on a summer spent
mapping rooftop solar for the TraceTheSun pilot, and the questions that turned out to
matter more than the map.

<!-- more -->

I did not have a technical background when I started at Open Energy Transition this
summer. What drew me to the internship was simple: I wanted hands-on experience in
environmental economics and renewable energy. I came in with a project idea that had a
rooftop-solar-mapping component, and no real sense of how many questions could be
hiding behind a single roof.

The first challenge was just learning how to look at one.

## Learning to see

One of the first tools I had to learn was JOSM. The mapping felt very technical at
first, in a way I was not used to: a new interface, unfamiliar imagery, rules to
follow, and the constant doubt about whether I was doing it right.

That feeling did not last long. Once I understood the process, I loved it. It was fun,
and also genuinely satisfying, to work feature by feature and slowly turn what I could
see in a satellite image into structured information. At some point mapping stopped
being just another internship task. I would find myself back in the OpenStreetMap
editor after working hours, sometimes while sitting with friends, simply because I
enjoyed it.

That was the first small, half-joking lesson of the summer: technical work is often
only as unapproachable as it looks before you learn how to approach it.

The same thing happened later with GitHub. Until this summer I had filed GitHub away as
something for computer scientists and software engineers. When I was asked to make
changes to the project website through its repository, I panicked. I remember wondering
how I could touch anything without breaking the whole site.

After a while I knew enough to edit the Markdown files and open my first pull request.
The contribution itself was tiny. But it shifted how I saw technical work. Not knowing
something does not mean you cannot do it. Sometimes the only missing piece is someone
willing to let you try.

## A map that poses questions

Learning the tools, though, was the least interesting part of mapping. The interesting
part was realising what the finished maps could tell us.

Before this internship I would probably have described Pakistan's energy problem
simply: there is not enough renewable energy, and solar has enormous potential. That is
still true. It is also incomplete.

The transition is about more than installed capacity. It is about how that capacity is
installed and by whom, how fast it is growing, who gets to take part, and what it means
for the electricity system when a large share of generation becomes decentralised.

This is where mapping becomes more than a technical exercise. A map can reveal patterns
that numbers alone do not show.

Some of those patterns stood out when I looked at Lahore. Solar adoption was clearly
concentrated in a few wealthy neighbourhoods and looked completely different elsewhere
in the city. Institutional rules mattered too, even in affluent areas: some housing
societies were open to rooftop solar, others were not.

So the real question is not simply why some people adopt solar and others do not.
Participation in the energy transition is not set by household preference alone. It is
shaped by income, housing situation, institutional rules, access to information, and
the surrounding infrastructure.

Seen this way, a rooftop solar map is also a map of something else.

## What we don't know

This matters even more in a country like Pakistan, where even the basic numbers on
solar capacity are uncertain.

That was one of the most interesting parts of the internship. We tend to talk as
though Pakistan's solar transition is slow and well understood. It is neither that
simple. Growth is decentralised, data is fragmented, and estimates of the actual
installed capacity vary widely.

That creates a strange problem: if you do not know what has already changed, how do you
plan for the change still to come?

The gap in information becomes a constraint in its own right, affecting planning, grid
management, forecasting, and any understanding of who benefits from the transition.

This idea stayed with me all summer. Data is not a neutral backdrop to policy. What we
know, and what we do not, decides what becomes visible first.

## Beyond the map

Solar Expo 2026 was one of the experiences that connected these questions to the real
world.

Until then, most of what I knew about the solar sector came from imagery, maps,
research papers, and discussions about adoption. At the expo the scale of the industry
felt tangible. We met solar suppliers and saw an industry scrambling to meet growing
demand. It made the transition harder to overlook.

There were households making choices, companies responding to the market, institutions
granting or denying opportunities, and a national grid trying to adjust to all of it.
In between sat researchers trying to make sense of it with the data they had.

None of these pieces stands alone. That was one of the things I found most
intellectually stimulating. A project that looks, at first, like detecting solar panels
from the air turns out to involve economics, governance, behaviour, infrastructure,
equity, and data.

The lines between disciplines started to feel much less firm. A mapping problem becomes
a data problem. A data problem becomes a governance problem. A question about adoption
becomes a question about affordability and inequality. A technical estimate ends up
shaping decisions well beyond the system that produced it.

## What comes next

I joined the internship partly to test whether this was a field I could see myself
studying further. I am leaving without a tidy answer, but with something more useful: a
much clearer sense of the questions I want to keep asking.

I am still not a programmer, a remote sensing specialist, or an energy systems
modeller. But the summer left me less convinced that complex problems should be tackled
inside disciplinary boundaries.

It also changed how I think about expertise. Technical knowledge has real value. So
does being able to ask what a technical result actually means. Who does it represent?
What does it miss? What assumptions sit behind it? And what decisions might be made
because of it?

I started the summer expecting to learn how rooftop solar could be mapped. What I found
was that mapping rooftops was only the beginning. The more interesting questions were
everything that came after: what those rooftops reveal about the people beneath them,
the institutions around them, and the energy transition they are quietly becoming part
of.
